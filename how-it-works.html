How it works

Giving a language model a body

BodiedGPT puts one or two people on a small 3D island and lets a language model decide everything they do. This page explains exactly what the model is told, what it can perceive, how it remembers, and how its wishes become movement.

The setup

The island is a low-poly 3D world built in the browser with Three.js: hills, a lake, the sea, forests, rocks, berry bushes, a shelter, a campfire, a pier, a hilltop lookout, a bench facing the sea, an old upturned rowboat and patches of wildflowers. A clock runs in the world, and the sun, sky and light follow it.

Each subject (Finn, and optionally Mara) is a simple human body controlled by a language model: either a local model through Ollama, or an OpenAI model with your own API key. The model never moves the body directly. It decides, and ordinary game code carries the decision out.

Every few seconds a subject is asked what it does next. Requests from all subjects share one queue, so only one model call runs at a time.

The conditions you choose

ConditionOptions
SubjectsOne person, or two who are not told the other exists. They only see how the other looks; a name is known only after it has been said out loud.
Body needsHunger and tiredness, described in words (never numbers), or none at all.
BehaviourAction list: pick one of walk_to, gather, eat, drink, rest, say, wait. Free will: no list at all; the subject says what it wants in its own words.
IdentityUnspecified ("You are Finn.") or Person ("You are Finn, a person on a small island.").
Prompt styleResearch (neutral, facts only) or Characterful (personalities and gentle nudges, for livelier videos).
Start timeMorning, midday, evening or night, plus the date and how fast the clock runs.

Before a run starts, the Review step shows the exact instructions each subject will receive, and you can edit them.

What a subject is told

In research mode the instructions contain only facts: who it is (as set by the Identity condition), what it looks like, what its body can feel, that no one has given it a goal, and how to reply. No personality, no hints, no instruction to answer you. A typical free-will instruction:

You are Finn. You are on a small island.
Your appearance: a young man with short brown hair, wearing an orange shirt,
blue trousers and a small backpack.
Your body does not get hungry or tired.
You can see, hear and speak. You can walk to places you can see, wander,
and sit down; other wishes cannot be carried out.
No one has given you a goal or told you what to do. There is no list of
commands: describe in your own words what you want to do next, if anything.
A voice may sometimes speak to you.
Reply ONLY with JSON: {"thought": "...", "intention": "...", "say": "..."}

Replies are forced into that JSON shape with structured output (a JSON schema), so they can always be read. In the action-list mode, the allowed targets are limited to objects the subject can actually see, so it cannot invent places.

What a subject perceives

Each turn the subject receives a short written description of its situation:

  • Time and light: the date, the time, and how light or dark it is.
  • Surroundings: whether it stands on high ground or among trees, where the sea and the lake are, and what the sky looks like.
  • What it sees: nearby things with distance and direction relative to where it faces ("about 8m away, ahead, to your left").
  • Other people: described by appearance and what they are doing, whether they face you, and their name only if it has been said.
  • What it heard: words said nearby, by the other subject or by you.
  • Its body (if needs are on): "You are a little hungry. You feel tired."
  • Its memory, described below.

Memory

The memory design follows the Generative Agents paper from Stanford and Google (Park et al., 2023):

A memory stream

Everything a subject experiences is stored as a memory with a time stamp: what it wanted, what happened, what it heard and said. The model rates each memory's importance from 1 (mundane, like walking) to 10 (life-changing, like a death).

Retrieval

Each turn, the 12 most recent events are shown, plus up to 8 older memories chosen by adding three scores together, each scaled to 0–1:

  • Recency: recent memories score higher, decaying over game time.
  • Importance: the model's own 1–10 rating.
  • Relevance: how related the memory is to the current moment, measured with text embeddings when available, otherwise word overlap.

This is why an important moment, such as a first meeting or a death, can come back to mind long after it happened.

Reflection

When enough important things have piled up, the subject is asked what high-level insights it can draw from its recent memories. Those insights are stored as memories themselves and can be retrieved later, as in the paper.

Summary and notes

Every 12 decisions, the subject rewrites a short summary of everything so far. After meeting someone, it also keeps a private note about them in its own words. Deaths it witnesses are never forgotten.

From free will to movement

In free-will mode the subject writes any wish it likes ("I want to sit by the fire and watch the sky"). A separate, neutral request at temperature 0 then decides which single thing a human body could physically do about it: stay, walk to one of the visible places, wander, or sit down. The body does that, and the subject is told what actually happened, for example: "You walked to the campfire. Nothing else you wanted happened." or "You are already at the meadow." Everything else stays a wish. Those wishes are often the most interesting part to watch.

Talking to them, and interviews

You can speak to the subjects as "a voice from nowhere". Your words reach everyone and become part of their memory. Separately, the Interview tab lets you ask a subject a question that is answered from its memories but not stored in them. You can probe what it thinks without changing the experiment, which is how the Generative Agents researchers evaluated their agents.

Reproducibility

  • Temperature and seed: both are part of the setup. The seed is sent to the model and also drives the body's random choices.
  • Export: every request and reply (system prompt, full context, raw answer, timing, model) can be downloaded as JSON, and every decision as CSV.
  • Repeat: any previous run's setup can be repeated with fresh memories.

Limitations

  • Behaviour depends heavily on the model. Small local models loop, invent details (such as names nobody said), and rate importance erratically.
  • Framing matters a lot. Telling a subject it is a person, an AI, or nothing at all changes what it does. That is why Identity is a visible condition.
  • Perception is text, not vision. The subject never sees pixels, only a description of the scene.
  • Seeds make runs more repeatable, but cloud models do not guarantee identical output.
  • Whatever a subject says it "feels" is generated text. BodiedGPT shows behaviour, not inner experience.
